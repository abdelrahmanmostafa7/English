Part 1

Grammar Foundations

Before individual rules (tenses, articles, modals), English has a small set of building blocks. This part defines the pieces of a sentence and how they fit together — the foundation for every later chapter.

What a sentence needs

A typical English sentence names something (the subject) and says something about it (the predicate). The predicate always contains a verb. Other pieces — objects, complements, adverbials — add meaning.

A1A2B1

Subject

A1A2B1

The subject is who or what the sentence is about. It usually comes before the verb in statements. Subjects can be nouns, pronouns, noun phrases, gerunds, or noun clauses.

Form

Subject + Verb (+ …)

When to use it

  • To name the doer or experiencer of the action
  • To name the thing being described (with linking verbs)
  • In questions, the subject often follows the auxiliary: Are you ready?

Examples

  • The product manager approved the release.
  • She works remotely twice a week.
  • Debugging this API takes patience. (gerund phrase as subject)
  • What they decided surprised the team. (noun clause as subject)

Common mistakes

Is raining. → It is raining.
English needs a subject; use dummy it for weather.

In the meeting was discussed the budget. → The budget was discussed in the meeting.
Avoid subject-less passive calques.

Verb (finite vs non-finite)

A2B1B2

A finite verb shows tense and agrees with the subject (works, worked, is working). A non-finite verb does not show tense by itself: infinitives (to work), gerunds (working), and participles (working / worked). Every complete sentence needs at least one finite verb (or an imperative, where the subject you is understood).

Examples

  • Finite: She reviews pull requests every morning.
  • Non-finite: She wants to review the PR. / Reviewing code is part of her job.

Common mistakes

She to review the code. → She reviews the code. / She wants to review the code.

Predicate

A2B1

The predicate is everything in the clause except the subject — typically the verb plus objects, complements, and adverbials.

Examples

  • The engineers deployed the hotfix before midnight.
  • The standup was unusually short.

Objects and complements

Direct object

A1A2

A direct object receives the action of a transitive verb. Ask: Verb + what/whom?

Form

Subject + Transitive Verb + Direct Object

Examples

  • We shipped the feature.
  • Please email the client.
  • She fixed three bugs before lunch.

Indirect object

A2B1

An indirect object is the person/thing that receives the direct object (often the beneficiary).

Form

S + V + Indirect Object + Direct Object OR S + V + Direct Object + to/for + Indirect Object

Examples

  • She sent the team a summary.
  • She sent a summary to the team.
  • Can you get me the latest build?

Compare

Give him the keys = Give the keys to him. With pronouns, English prefers: Give them to him (not usually Give him them in careful usage).

Subject complement

A2B1

After linking verbs (be, seem, become, feel, look, sound, remain), a subject complement renames or describes the subject. It is not an object.

Form

S + Linking Verb + Subject Complement (noun / adjective / phrase)

Examples

  • The meeting was productive. (adjective)
  • Aisha became the tech lead. (noun phrase)
  • The logs look incomplete.

Common mistakes

She is engineer. → She is an engineer.
Articles still apply after linking be.

The solution seems correctly. → The solution seems correct.
Use an adjective, not an adverb, after linking verbs.

Object complement

B1B2

An object complement follows the object and completes its meaning — often after verbs like make, call, consider, appoint, find, elect.

Form

S + V + Object + Object Complement

Examples

  • They appointed her engineering manager.
  • The board found the proposal unrealistic.
  • We call this pattern dependency injection.

Clauses and phrases

Phrase vs clause

A2B1

A phrase is a group of words without a finite verb acting as one unit (noun phrase, prepositional phrase, verb phrase). A clause has a subject–predicate structure (at least in full clauses).

Examples

  • Phrase: after the sprint review
  • Clause: after we finished the sprint review

Independent (main) clause

A2B1

An independent clause can stand alone as a sentence. It has a subject and a finite verb and expresses a complete idea.

Examples

  • The deploy succeeded.
  • We postponed the launch, and the marketing team updated the landing page.

Dependent (subordinate) clause

B1B2

A dependent clause cannot stand alone. It needs a main clause. Common types: noun clauses, relative (adjective) clauses, adverb clauses.

Examples

  • If the tests fail, we roll back. (If the tests fail = dependent)
  • The engineer who wrote the migration is on leave.
  • I know that the deadline is Friday.

Auxiliary vs main verbs

Main verbs carry the core meaning (ship, decide, need). Auxiliary (helping) verbs build tense, aspect, voice, modality, and questions/negatives: be, have, do and the modals (can, will, must…).

Examples

  • She is reviewing the PR. (is = auxiliary; reviewing = main)
  • They have finished the migration.
  • Do you agree with the estimate?
  • We can ship tomorrow.

Basic word order in statements

A1A2B1

Default English word order is Subject–Verb–Object (SVO). Adverbials of place/time often come at the end; frequency adverbs often take mid-position (see Part 7 and Part 23).

Form

Subject + (auxiliary) + Verb + Object + (place) + (time)

Examples

  • The team shipped the patch yesterday.
  • She often joins standups from Cairo.

Common mistakes

Yesterday shipped the team the patch. → The team shipped the patch yesterday.

She joins often standups. → She often joins standups.

Warning

Do not drop the subject in full sentences (common when translating from pro-drop languages): Works from home → She works from home.

Finite clauses need tense marking

Every full declarative/interrogative sentence needs a finite verb (tense-marked), except imperatives with an implied you.

Examples

  • Ship the fix. (imperative — subject understood)
  • Please be careful with production credentials.
  • It is essential that she be present. (subjunctive — advanced; see formal AmE/BrE)

Complement vs object — quick test

B1B2

If you can turn the sentence into a passive with the same noun as subject, it was likely an object: They fixed the bug → The bug was fixed. Subject complements do not passivise that way: She became lead ↛ *Lead was become by her.

Nouns name people, places, things, ideas, and events. English noun grammar hinges on countability, articles, plurals, and how nouns combine into complex noun phrases.

Countable vs uncountable

A1A2B1

Countable nouns can be singular or plural and take a/an and numbers. Uncountable nouns are not normally pluralised and do not take a/an. They take singular verbs and quantifiers like some, much, a little, a piece of.

Examples

  • Countable: a ticket / two tickets; a bug / several bugs
  • Uncountable: information, advice, software, feedback, progress, luggage, furniture
  • We need more information. (not informations)
  • She gave me some useful advice. (not an advice)

Common mistakes

an information → some information / a piece of information

many softwares → a lot of software / several software products

homeworks → homework / three homework assignments

a feedback → some feedback / a piece of feedback

Collective nouns

B1B2

Collective nouns name a group. In American English they usually take a singular verb when the group acts as one unit (The team is ready). British English often allows plural (The team are arguing) when members act individually.

Examples

  • The committee has approved the budget. (AmE / unit)
  • The staff are working from different time zones. (BrE / individuals)

Possession

Possessive 's and of-phrases

A2B1

Use 's especially with people, animals, time expressions, and organisations. Use of with things, long noun phrases, and many abstract relationships.

Form

noun + 's + noun · of + noun phrase

When to use it

  • Singular nouns: engineer → engineer's
  • Plural ending in s: engineers → engineers'
  • Irregular plural: children's, people's
  • Names ending in s: James's or James' (style choice; be consistent)

Examples

  • the company's roadmap / the roadmap of the company
  • yesterday's meeting / a week's notice
  • the colour of the button (not usually the button's colour in formal writing, though both occur)
  • the CEO's decision

Double possessive

B1B2

English allows a friend of mine / a colleague of Sara's — combining of with a possessive. It often means 'one among several' and sounds natural with indefinite determiners.

Examples

  • A friend of mine recommended this library.
  • That idea of Nora's actually solved the bottleneck.

Noun modifiers and complex noun phrases

English often stacks nouns as modifiers: user authentication flow, production database migration plan. The last noun is the head; earlier nouns modify it. Do not pluralise modifiers unless the plural meaning is required (a systems engineer vs a system engineer — both exist; check collocation).

Examples

  • customer support ticket
  • end-to-end encryption key
  • quarterly performance review

Nominalization

B2C1

Nominalization turns verbs/adjectives into nouns (decide → decision, reliable → reliability). Useful in formal writing, but overuse makes prose heavy. Prefer verbs when clarity matters.

Examples

  • We decided to delay the launch. (clearer)
  • The decision to delay the launch was announced yesterday. (more formal/noun-heavy)

Note

Normally uncountable (learn these): advice, information, news, furniture, luggage/baggage, equipment, software, homework, knowledge, progress, research, evidence, traffic, weather, accommodation (BrE often uncountable).

Use partitives: a piece of advice, an item of news, two pieces of luggage, a research paper.

Pronouns replace noun phrases to avoid repetition. Accuracy depends on case (subject/object), agreement, clear reference, and register (especially with generic they and formal alternatives).

Possessive adjectives vs possessive pronouns

A1A2

Possessive adjectives (my, your, their) come before a noun. Possessive pronouns (mine, yours, theirs) stand alone and replace a noun phrase.

Examples

  • This is my laptop. This laptop is mine.
  • Is this seat yours?

Reflexive pronouns

A2B1B2

Use reflexives when the object refers to the same entity as the subject, or for emphasis (I myself…). Do not use them as a substitute for subject/object pronouns in coordination.

Examples

  • She taught herself TypeScript.
  • Please help yourself to coffee.
  • I fixed it myself. (emphasis)

Common mistakes

Please contact myself. → Please contact me.

If you have questions, ask John or myself. → …ask John or me.

Reciprocal pronouns

B1

Each other and one another express mutual action. In modern usage they are largely interchangeable; some writers prefer one another for more than two, but this is not a hard rule.

Examples

  • The two services call each other every few seconds.
  • Team members trust one another.

Demonstratives, indefinites, interrogatives

Demonstrative pronouns

A1A2

This/these (near), that/those (far) can be determiners or pronouns.

Examples

  • This is the latest build. Those were outdated screenshots.
  • I prefer this approach to that.

Indefinite pronouns

A2B1B2

Words like someone, anybody, nothing, everything, each, either, neither, both, few, many, one refer to non-specific people/things. Most compounds with every-/some-/any-/no- take singular verbs.

Examples

  • Everyone is online. (singular verb)
  • Nothing was broken in production.
  • Someone left their laptop. (singular they — natural modern English)

Common mistakes

Everyone are ready. → Everyone is ready.

I don't know nothing. → I don't know anything. / I know nothing.

One / ones

A2B1

One/ones substitute for countable nouns to avoid repetition.

Examples

  • Which ticket do you mean — the urgent one?
  • We need smaller monitors; these ones are too large.

Dummy it and existential there

Dummy it

A1A2B1

English uses it as an empty subject for weather, time, distance, and extraposed clauses.

Examples

  • It is raining in London.
  • It is 3 p.m. in Cairo.
  • It is important to document the API.
  • It seems that the cache is stale.

Existential there

A1A2

There is/are introduces existence or presence. Agreement usually follows the real noun phrase (though spoken English often uses there's + plural).

Examples

  • There is a bug in the payment flow.
  • There are three open issues.
  • SPOKEN There's two problems… (common; write There are in formal prose)

Pronoun reference and agreement

Warning

A pronoun must point clearly to an antecedent. Ambiguity is a C1 writing error:

When Maya told Hana the news, she was shocked. (Who was shocked?)

When Maya told Hana the news, Hana was shocked.

Advanced note

Generic pronouns: Older formal style used he generically; modern standard English uses singular they, or rephrases with plurals (users… they). He or she is possible but often clumsy.

FORMAL One should save one's work frequently. (academic/old-fashioned in AmE; more natural in BrE formal writing)

Advanced pronoun reference

Ambiguity, cataphora, and implied antecedents

B2C1C2

Pronouns may point forward (cataphora) in skilled writing, but clarity comes first.

Examples

  • When she finally spoke, Maya rejected the proposal. (cataphora — OK if stylistic)
  • They should update the docs. (Who? Prefer a noun if unclear)
  • This is frustrating. (Prefer: This delay is frustrating.)

one as generic pronoun

C1

FORMAL Especially BrE academic: One should save one's work. AmE often finds this stiff; prefer you or rephrase.

Determiners

Determiners come at the start of a noun phrase and specify reference and quantity (articles, demonstratives, possessives, quantifiers, numbers, distributives). English allows a limited, ordered stack of determiners.

Determiner order (pre-, central, post-)

B1B2C1

English noun phrases allow: predeterminers → central determiners → postdeterminers → adjectives → noun.

Form

all/both/half/double/such/what/quite/rather → article/demonstrative/possessive → numbers/many/few/other → Adj → Noun

  • both these approaches

  • twice the cost
  • such a difficult bug
  • what a mess
  • the first two sprints
  • my many unanswered emails

my this laptop → this laptop of mine / this laptop

these my colleagues → these colleagues of mine / my colleagues

Predeterminers in detail

all / both / half

A2B1

These can appear before determiners or before pronouns (all of them). Both is for exactly two.

Examples

  • All (of) the tests passed.
  • Both (of) the designs look clean.
  • Half (of) the budget is gone.
  • All of us agreed. / We all agreed.

such / what (exclamative) / rather / quite

B1B2

Such and exclamative what often need a/an with singular count nouns. Quite/rather a + adjective + noun is common in BrE.

Examples

  • It was such a clear explanation.
  • What a useful library!
  • It was rather a long meeting. BrE
  • She's quite a skilled negotiator.

A2B1

Each and every take singular nouns and usually singular verbs. Either/neither refer to two options.

Quantifier details learners miss

some / any / no

A2B1

Some is common in affirmatives and polite offers/requests. Any is common in negatives and many questions. No + noun is a determiner alternative to not any.

Examples

  • Would you like some coffee? (expecting yes)
  • Do you have any questions? / I don't have any questions.
  • There is no easy fix.
  • Any engineer can join the on-call rotation. (any = every / whichever)

enough / too / plenty of

A2B1

Enough comes after adjectives/adverbs but before nouns: clear enough / enough time.

Examples

  • The docs aren't clear enough.
  • We don't have enough reviewers.
  • The query is too slow for production.
  • There's plenty of coffee in the kitchen.

money enough → enough money
Enough usually precedes nouns.

another / other / others / the other / the others

These mark remaining or additional referents and are easy to confuse.

Examples

  • Open another ticket. (one more, unspecified)
  • Other tickets can wait.
  • Some tests passed; others failed.
  • We have two options. The other is riskier.
  • One service is down; the others are healthy.

Part 5

Articles

English has three article choices: a/an (indefinite), the (definite), and the zero article. Articles encode whether a noun is known, unique, generic, or newly introduced. This is one of the hardest systems for learners.

A / An — indefinite article

Form of a / an

A1A2

Use a before consonant sounds and an before vowel sounds — based on pronunciation, not spelling.

Examples

  • a user, a university, a European office (consonant /j/ sound)
  • an hour, an honour, an MBA, an HTTP header (vowel sound)
  • an API / a REST API (depends on how you pronounce the initialism)

Main uses of a/an

A1A2B1

Introduce a singular countable noun when it is not uniquely identified for the listener.

When to use it

  • First mention: I found a bug in checkout.
  • Jobs and roles: She is a product designer.
  • Classifications: A whale is a mammal. (also with the for species — see below)
  • Frequency: twice a week, 80 kilometres an hour
  • Rates: $50 a ticket
  • Exclamations: What a day!

Examples

  • We need a staging environment.
  • He works as an SRE.
  • They meet three times a month.

Common mistakes

She is engineer. → She is an engineer.

I have a information. → I have some information.

The — definite article

Core meaning of the

A1A2B1

Use the when the listener can identify the referent — because it was mentioned, is unique, is shared knowledge, or is defined by a modifier.

When to use it

  • Second mention: I found a bug… The bug blocked payments.
  • Unique in context: Please close the door.
  • Shared knowledge: the sun, the internet, the CEO (of our company)
  • Superlatives and ordinals: the best option, the first release
  • Defining modifiers: the engineer who wrote the migration

Zero article

When English uses no article

A2B1B2

Zero article is common with plural and uncountable nouns in a general sense, and with many institutional/transport/meal patterns.

When to use it

  • Plural generics: Engineers need clear requirements.
  • Uncountable generics: Software evolves quickly.
  • Abstract concepts: Trust matters in remote teams.
  • Languages & subjects: She studies Arabic and computer science.
  • Sports & games: They play football.
  • Meals (general): after lunch (but the lunch we had with the client)
  • Transport (by): by train, by bus, by email
  • Titles with names: Doctor Hassan, President Lee (but the doctor as role)
  • Home/work/bed patterns: at home, at work, in bed

Examples

  • Privacy is a product requirement.
  • She goes to school by metro.
  • Dinner is at eight.

Common mistakes

The life is complicated. → Life is complicated.

She went to the home. → She went home.

I like the music. → I like music.
Unless a specific set of music is meant.

Advanced note

Institutions: in hospital (BrE, as patient) vs in the hospital (AmE or visiting the building). at university (BrE) vs in college (AmE patterns). Learn collocations; do not overgeneralise.

Geographic the: the Sudan, the Gambia, the Congo appear in some styles; modern usage often drops the for country names except established forms (the Netherlands, the United States).

Compare

A product manager needs empathy. (any / role classification)
The product manager needs empathy. (our known PM / the role as defined in context)
Product managers need empathy. (generic plural — very natural)

Part 6

Adjectives

Adjectives describe nouns and pronouns. Key issues: position (attributive vs predicative), order before nouns, gradability, adjective–preposition patterns, and comparison (see also Part 19).

Attributive vs predicative

A2B1B2

Attributive adjectives appear before a noun (a critical bug). Predicative adjectives appear after linking verbs (The bug is critical). Some adjectives are mainly one or the other: the main reason (not usually the reason is main); she is asleep (not an asleep engineer).

Examples

  • a reliable service / The service is reliable.
  • the only solution / This is the only solution.
  • The baby is awake. (not an awake baby in careful style — prefer a baby who is awake)

Adjective order

B1B2

When several adjectives appear before a noun, English prefers a conventional order. Treat this as a strong tendency, not an absolute law.

Form

opinion → size → age → shape → colour → origin → material → purpose + NOUN

Examples

  • a reliable old production server
  • an innovative young Egyptian software engineer

Note

Native speakers rarely stack more than two or three adjectives. Prefer a small, reliable production server.

B1B2

Examples

  • absolutely essential (not usually very essential)
  • completely finished / totally wrong

Stative vs dynamic verbs

Stative verbs

A2B1B2

Stative verbs describe states (not actions): mental states, emotions, possession, senses, and some relating verbs. They are not normally used in continuous (-ing) forms when they keep a stative meaning.

When to use it

  • Cognition: know, believe, understand, remember, forget, mean, realise, suppose, agree, disagree, doubt
  • Emotion/preference: like, love, hate, prefer, want, need, wish
  • Possession/relation: have (possession), own, belong, include, contain, consist of, seem, appear, look (=seem), sound, resemble
  • Senses (involuntary): hear, see, smell, taste (when not deliberate actions)

Examples

  • I agree with the estimate. NOT I am agree. / I am agreeing. (usually)
  • She knows Kubernetes well.
  • This repo belongs to the platform team.
  • The plan seems solid.

Common mistakes

I am agree. → I agree.
Agree is stative; also never am agree — missing adjective/verb form.

I am knowing the answer. → I know the answer.

She is wanting a new laptop. → She wants a new laptop.

Advanced note

Some normally stative verbs become dynamic when they describe temporary behaviour or deliberate actions:

  • I'm seeing the client at 3 (meeting) vs I see what you mean (understand).
  • He's having a meeting / having lunch vs He has two laptops.
  • I'm loving this new IDE INFORMAL advertising style — still marked.
  • What are you thinking about? (active mental process) vs I think you're right (opinion).

Common mistakes

The design looks well. → The design looks good.
Unless referring to eyesight ability.

She became angrily. → She became angry.

  • Zero: If you heat ice, it melts. / If the tests fail, the pipeline stops.

B1B2C1

These introduce conditions with nuance.

Examples

  • Unless we get approval, we can't launch. (= if we do not)
  • Provided (that) / Providing (that) the tests pass, we deploy.
  • As long as the API stays stable, clients won't notice.
  • Take an umbrella in case it rains. (precaution — not the same as if)
  • Even if we rush, we won't finish tonight. (concession)
  • Only if we get two approvals can we proceed. (restrictive; may trigger inversion)

B1B2

Wish about present: past simple. Wish about past: past perfect. Wish about annoying habits: would. Wish + would not for first-person ability wishes (*I wish I would… is usually wrong).

Examples

  • I wish we had better monitoring. (present unreal)
  • I wish I had asked sooner. (past regret)
  • I wish the alert would stop firing. (others' behaviour)
  • If only we had known!

Common mistakes

I wish I will have more time. → I wish I had more time.

I wish I would be taller. → I wish I were / was taller.

Inverted conditionals (formal)

C1C2

In formal/written English, if can be omitted with inversion: Had I known…, Were I…, Should you need…

Examples

  • Had I known about the outage, I would have joined earlier.
  • Were I you, I'd postpone the release. (formal)
  • Should you need help, ping me on Slack.
  • Were it not for the backup, we would have lost everything.

Real vs unreal — decision guide

  • Ask: Is the condition possible/open? → Zero/First (real).
  • Ask: Is it hypothetical now/future? → Second (unreal present/future).
  • Ask: Are you imagining a different past? → Third (unreal past).
  • Ask: Does a past unreal condition affect now? → Mixed (had + would).

if + should / happen to

C1C2

Makes conditions less likely or more tentative — useful in polite instructions.

Examples

  • If you should see a Sev-1, page me immediately.
  • If you happen to find the logs, send them over.
  • Should the build fail, revert the commit. (inversion)

will / would in if-clauses

C1

Normally avoid will after if for time reference. Exceptions: willingness, polite insistence, or result meaning.

Examples

  • If you will wait here, I'll get the key. (willingness)
  • If you would take a seat… (polite)
  • If it will make you happier, we can add logging. (result/willingness reading)

Part 16

Questions

English questions rely on auxiliary verbs and word order. Subject questions look different from object questions — a major source of mistakes.

Yes/No questions

A1A2

Invert auxiliary/modal/be with the subject. If there is no auxiliary, insert do/does/did.

Examples

  • Are you on-call? / Can they join? / Have you eaten?
  • Do you use Vim? / Did the tests pass?

Wh- questions

A1A2B1

Wh-word + auxiliary + subject + main verb… (for object/adverbial questions).

Examples

  • Where do you work?
  • What did she say?
  • How long have you been waiting?
  • Why is the pipeline red?

Subject vs object questions

A2B1B2

If the wh-word is the subject, do not use do-support; keep statement order after the wh-word.

Examples

  • Object: Who did you see? (you saw X)
  • Subject: Who saw you? (X saw you)
  • Object: What did the alert trigger?
  • Subject: What triggered the alert?
  • Which engineer fixed it? (subject)
  • Which engineer did you call? (object)

Common mistakes

Who did see you? → Who saw you?

Who you saw? → Who did you see?

Indirect / embedded questions

B1B2

Inside statements or polite requests, use statement order. No do-inversion after the wh-word/if.

Examples

  • I wonder where the logs are.
  • Could you tell me what time it starts?
  • Do you know if the VPN is down?

Common mistakes

Could you tell me where is the office? → Could you tell me where the office is?

Question tags

B1B2

Tag matches the auxiliary and reverses polarity. Positive statement → negative tag (usually), and vice versa. Intonation: falling = expect agreement; rising = genuine check.

Examples

  • You're on-call, aren't you?
  • She can't join, can she?
  • Let's ship, shall we? (special)
  • I'm late, aren't I? (special)

Negative, rhetorical, and echo questions

B2C1

Negative questions can express surprise or expectation: Isn't that the old endpoint? Rhetorical questions don't expect answers. Echo questions check what was heard: You deleted what?

Examples

  • Haven't we already fixed this?
  • Who cares about the legacy stylesheet?
  • She's joining when?

Special question patterns

Prepositions in questions

B1B2C1

Informal English strands prepositions; formal English may front them with whom/which.

Examples

  • Who did you give the keys to?
  • To whom did you give the keys? FORMAL
  • What are you looking at?
  • Which service does this depend on?

Negative questions

B1B2

Express surprise, confirmation, or soft persuasion.

Examples

  • Isn't this the same bug as last week?
  • Why don't we postpone?
  • Haven't you finished yet?

Note

Answer negative questions carefully: in English, Yes affirms the positive fact. If someone asks Aren't you on-call? and you are, answer Yes (I am).

Negation

English negation uses not, negative determiners/pronouns (no, nothing), and negative adverbs (never, hardly). Word order, scope, and inversion matter at higher levels.

not with auxiliaries

A1A2

Place not (n't) after the first auxiliary/modal/be. Use do-support with simple present/past lexical verbs.

Examples

  • She is not available. / She isn't available.

  • They do not agree. / They don't agree.
  • I did not approve that PR.

A2B1

no + noun; not + article/determiner phrase. Negative pronouns already negate — avoid double negation in standard English.

Examples

  • I have no idea. / I don't have any idea.
  • Nobody called. / I didn't see anyone.
  • Nothing changed. / I didn't change anything.

I don't know nothing. → I don't know anything. / I know nothing.

Clauses

Clauses are the main way English builds complex meaning: noun clauses as subjects/objects, relative clauses as noun modifiers, and adverb clauses as circumstance markers.

Noun clauses

Noun (content) clauses

B1B2

Introduced by that, if/whether, wh-words. They act as subjects, objects, or complements.

Examples

  • I know that the deadline is Friday.
  • Whether we ship depends on QA.
  • What we need is clearer ownership. (also cleft-like)
  • The question is why latency spiked.

Defining vs non-defining

B1B2

  • Non-defining: Our CTO, who joined in 2019, prefers Rust.

Our CTO who joined in 2019 prefers Rust. → Add commas if there is only one CTO.

The API, that we deprecated, still gets traffic. → Use which (or who) after a comma — not that.

Part 19

Comparison

Comparison covers comparative/superlative forms, equality structures, modifiers of degree, and advanced correlative patterns.

Forming comparatives and superlatives

A2B1

One-syllable: -er/-est. Two syllables: often -er or more; -y → ier. Three+: more/most. Irregular: good/better/best; bad/worse/worst; far/farther|further; little/less/least; many|much/more/most.

Examples

  • faster, simpler, happier, more reliable, most secure
  • This build is better than the last one.
  • She is the most careful reviewer on the team.

Common mistakes

more faster → faster / much faster

the most unique → unique / truly unique
Avoid grading absolute adjectives carelessly.

Equality and inequality

A2B1

as… as for equality; not as/so… as for inequality. less/fewer for lower degree/quantity; more/most for higher.

Examples

  • Go is as fast as we need for this service.
  • The UI is not as intuitive as the old one.
  • fewer tickets / less downtime / less noise

Note

Careful usage: fewer with plurals, less with uncountables. Spoken English often uses less with plurals (less tickets) — marked in formal writing.

Modifying comparatives

B1B2

Intensify with much, far, a lot, way (informal), significantly. Soften with a bit, slightly, rather. Do not use very directly before a comparative (*very better).

Examples

  • much clearer, far more stable, a lot easier, slightly worse
  • The more we automate, the fewer incidents we see.

Advanced comparison patterns

B2C1

Correlative the… the…, double marking (usually avoided), progressive comparison with increasingly.

Examples

  • The sooner we decide, the better.
  • The more carefully you test, the fewer regressions you ship.
  • Increasingly complex systems need better observability.
  • This approach is becoming more and more popular.

Comparison structures in full

Examples

  • as many tickets as last week / as much traffic as last week
  • twice as fast as the old query / half as expensive
  • the same as / similar to / different from
  • no sooner… than… / hardly… when… (formal narrative)
  • She is senior to me. (note: senior/junior/superior + to, not than)

Common mistakes

more better → better

superior than → superior to

one of the best engineer → one of the best engineers

Absolute adjectives — careful grading

C1

Words like unique, perfect, empty, dead, complete are traditionally non-gradable. In real usage, people say almost unique / more complete. In careful formal writing, prefer almost/nearly/unique in X respect.

Participles & Participial Clauses

Participles (-ing / -ed / having + PP) create compact modifiers and adverbial clauses. They are powerful in formal writing and dangerous when dangling.

Present, past, and perfect participles

B1B2C1

Present participle: V-ing (active/in-progress). Past participle: V-ed/irregular (often passive/completed). Perfect participle: having + PP (earlier completion).

Examples

  • a growing codebase; growing quickly, the team hired more engineers
  • written documentation; Written in Rust, the service uses little memory
  • Having finished the migration, we deleted the legacy tables.

Participial adjectives

A2B1

Many participles act as adjectives: interesting/interested, surprising/surprised, frozen, dedicated.

Examples

  • a dedicated engineer; frozen requirements; a surprising result

Participial clauses

B2C1

Examples

  • Walking into the office, I noticed the monitors were off.

Emphasis & Advanced Structures

Clefts, fronting, inversion, emphatic do, substitution, and ellipsis help manage focus and avoid repetition — especially in C1–C2 writing and speaking.

Cleft sentences (it-clefts)

B2C1

It + be + highlighted element + relative clause. Used to focus one piece of information.

Examples

  • It was Nora who found the race condition.
  • It was the cache that caused the spike.
  • It was yesterday that we noticed the drift.

Pseudo-clefts (wh-clefts)

B2C1

What-clause + be + focus. Excellent for defining needs and correcting focus.

Examples

  • What we need is better observability.
  • What surprised me was the latency, not the CPU.
  • What you should do is open a Sev-2.

Inversion for emphasis

C1C2

After negative/limiting adverbials and in conditional inversion.

Examples

  • Never have we shipped without tests.
  • Only then did we understand the root cause.
  • Not until midnight did the alerts stop.
  • Had we known, we would have scaled up.

Emphatic do

B1B2

Use do/does/did + bare infinitive to emphasise affirmation or contrast.

Examples

  • I do understand your concern.
  • She did send the email — check spam.

Substitution and ellipsis

B2C1

Substitute with so, not, one/ones, do so, that; omit recoverable material in coordination and replies.

Examples

  • I think so. / I hope not.
  • She fixed the bug, and Maya did too.
  • Want coffee? — I might. (ellipsis)
  • If you can join, please do.

More emphasis devices

Examples

  • It is reliability that customers pay for.
  • What went wrong was the timeout configuration.

C1

Examples

  • SPOKEN You free at 5?
  • INFORMAL Just shipped. Looks good on my side.
  • STANDARD I've just shipped the fix. It looks good on my side.
  • FORMAL The fix has been deployed, and initial checks appear satisfactory.
  • RARE Seldom have we encountered such a failure mode.

Note

Naturalness is about collocation and information packaging, not only correctness. Read native professional writing in your field and notice repeated patterns.

Ellipsis and fragments that sound native

Examples

  • SPOKEN Ready? — Almost.
  • SPOKEN Coffee? — Please.
  • SPOKEN Serves me right for skipping tests.
  • INFORMAL Long story short, we rolled back.
  • STANDARD Looking forward to your feedback.
  • FORMAL Please find attached the revised proposal.

Note

Native speakers omit recoverable subjects/auxiliaries in notes and chat. In essays, interviews, and documentation, write full clauses unless the genre expects brevity.

Part 33

Common Error Index

Alphabetical WRONG → CORRECT → EXPLANATION entries for rapid correction lookup.

A

I am agree. → I agree.
Agree is a verb; no progressive be + agree in this meaning.
according to me → in my opinion / I think
According to + external source.
an advice → some advice / a piece of advice
Advice is uncountable.
although… but… → although… / …but…
Use one contrast marker, not both.
arrive to Cairo → arrive in Cairo
Arrive in + city; arrive at + point.

B

because of he was late → because he was late / because of his lateness
Because + clause; because of + noun.
been to / gone to confusion → She's gone to Paris (still there) / She's been to Paris (visited)
Gone vs been.

C

Can you tell me where is it? → Can you tell me where it is?
Embedded question = statement order.
discuss about → discuss
Discuss takes a direct object.
despite of → despite / in spite of
No of after despite.

D

depend of → depend on
Fixed preposition.
didn't went → didn't go
After did, use base form.
do a mistake → make a mistake
Collocation.

E

every people → everyone / every person / all people
Every + singular noun.
explain me → explain to me
Explain something to someone.

F

for get used → get used to
Be/get used to + noun/gerund.
from my side, I think → I think (drop filler)
Wordy non-native filler.

G

go to home → go home
No to/the with home in this pattern.
good in English → good at English
Good at + skill.

H

hardly work (meaning 'effort') → work hard
Hardly = almost not.
have reason → be right / have a reason
Calque; prefer you're right.

I

I have 25 years → I am 25 (years old)
Age with be.
if I will see him → if I see him
No will in future time/if-clauses.
in the night (habit) → at night
At night = habitual.
informations → information
Uncountable.

J

job as a work → job / work
Job countable; work uncountable.

K

know to swim → know how to swim
Know how + infinitive.

L

lay down (recline) → lie down
Lie = recline; lay needs object.
listen the music → listen to the music
Listen to.
look forward to meet → look forward to meeting
To = preposition.

M

married with → married to
Fixed preposition.
must to go → must go
Modal + bare infinitive.

N

news are → news is
News is singular.
no one don't know → no one knows
Already negative.

O

one of my friend → one of my friends
One of + plural.
open the light → turn on the light
Collocation.

P

people is → people are
People is plural.
prefer than → prefer X to Y
Prefer to, not than.

Q

quiet / quite mix → quiet = silent; quite = fairly/completely
Spelling/meaning.

R

recommend me a book → *recommend me that → recommend a book (to me) / recommend that I…
Pattern care.
return back → return / go back
Return already includes back.

S

said me → told me / said to me
Say vs tell.
sheeps → sheep
Zero plural.
since two years → for two years / since 2024
For duration; since point.
succeed to do → succeed in doing
Succeed in + gerund.
suggest to go → suggest going / suggest that we go
Suggest + gerund/that.

T

take a decision → make a decision (also take in some varieties)
Make is safest internationally.
the both options → both options / both of the options
Determiner order.
there have → there are / they have
Existential is/are.

U

until / by mix → by = deadline; until = continuing up to
See Part 8.
used to do vs be used to doing → past habit vs accustomed
See Part 29.

V

very better → much/far better
Don't use very + comparative.

W

when I will arrive → when I arrive
Present in future time clause.
worth to see → worth seeing
Worth + gerund.
Who did see you? → Who saw you?
Subject question.

Y

yesterday night → last night
Collocation.
